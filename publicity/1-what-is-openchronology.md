# OpenChronology — What Is OpenChronology? Script and Storyboard

**Runtime:** ~0:57 (the scratch narration's cut) · **Format:** animated infographic, **Paper** look ([audiences and series](0-audiences-and-series.md#3--one-visual-system-five-looks)) · **Aspect:** 16:9, title-safe for 1:1 and 9:16
**Audience:** everyone meeting OpenChronology for the first time, from a social feed, the website's home page or a shared link.
**Purpose:** the front door. One idea (any span of time, on one exact line you can zoom), three examples that show its range, and where to go next. Every other video can assume this one.

**How it differs from the rest:** it explains nothing in depth. Each example is one image held for a few seconds, and it ends by pointing at the other videos by audience.

**Production notes:**

- Narration is 127 words, about 140 wpm when read. Warm, plain and unhurried; no feature lists.
- **The motif:** one timeline axis that stays on screen the whole video. Its content changes with each example, but it never leaves, so the viewer understands it's one tool.
- **Accuracy guardrails:** "free" means free software under the GPL version 3 with no paid features; a server has per-account storage limits. Don't promise timescale.info sign-ups until they're open (**[CONFIRM]**).

## ACT 1 · ONE LINE OF TIME (0:00 – 0:35)

### Scene 1 · 0:00 – 0:12 · Everything happens in time

**FRAME:** The paper ground. A single horizontal axis draws across the frame. Above it, three small labels appear in turn, far apart: "A war", "A story", "A server's logs".

**MOTION:** The axis draws left to right; each label drops onto the axis as a dot with a short stem, on the word that names it.

**OST:** "Everything happens in time."

**VO:**
"Everything that matters happened at some point in time: a war, a story you're writing, the hour your server went down."

### Scene 2 · 0:12 – 0:35 · Zoom, and nothing blurs

**FRAME:** The axis fills with moments (dots with titles) and durations (bands). A zoom lens sweeps in: the labels on the ruler change from "1800 · 1900 · 2000" to "July 1969" to "20:17:40". A small exact fraction floats beside the ruler: "1/3 s".

**MOTION:** A continuous zoom into one moment; the ruler labels roll over smoothly from centuries to seconds; the fraction pops in at the end and stays.

**OST:** "Zoom from centuries to seconds."

**VO:**
"OpenChronology puts them on one line you can zoom, from billions of years down to a fraction of a second. Every time is stored exactly, as a fraction, so nothing blurs or rounds however far you go."

## ACT 2 · THREE TIMELINES (0:35 – 0:58)

### Scene 3 · 0:35 – 0:58 · History, a story, deep time

**FRAME:** Three panels across the frame, each a small timeline: (1) "The United States, 1776–today" with a Civil War band and an Apollo 11 dot; (2) "A story's timeline" with two coloured tracks labelled "Ash" and "Mirelle" and an arc between them; (3) "Deep time" with eon bands in rock colours and a tiny bright sliver at the right end labelled "all of human history".

**MOTION:** Panels slide in left to right on their cue words; in panel 3 a magnifier enlarges the sliver.

**OST:** "History. Stories. Deep time."

**VO:**
"Students follow a country's history. Writers keep their story's chronology straight. Teachers show four and a half billion years of Earth, and how thin the slice holding all of human history is."

## ACT 3 · YOURS (0:58 – 1:15)

### Scene 4 · 0:58 – 1:15 · Free, and where you want it

**FRAME:** Three icons in a row under the axis: a browser window ("Website"), a laptop ("Desktop app"), a single file ("Offline file"). Beneath them, a chip: "Free software · GPL v3". Then the end card: the ◷ openchronology wordmark, "timescale.info", and a line: "Next: How to see a timeline · and a video for what you do". The end card also gives the source: "github.com/openchronology/timeline-app".

**MOTION:** Icons pop in; the chip slides up; the end card fades in over them and holds.

**OST:** "Free. Online, on your desktop, or offline."

**VO:**
"It's free software. Use it on the website, in the desktop app, or as a single file that never touches the network. Start with how to read a timeline, then pick the video for what you do."

## Appendix C · Questions this audience is likely to ask

1. **Do I need an account?** No, to read public timelines or to make one in the browser and export it. See Video 3.
2. **Is it really free?** Yes; there are no payments. A server limits each account's storage.
3. **Can I see one now?** Link a public timeline under the video (the United States sample from Video 5 is a good first one).
