# OpenChronology — Tour: Abraham Lincoln, 1809–1865. Script and Storyboard

**Runtime:** ~4:00 (at 140 wpm, with pauses) · **Format:** the real app, captured, in a browser frame, with infographic callouts; **Archive** look · **Aspect:** 16:9
**Audience:** students and history teachers, biography readers, and anyone who knows Lincoln as a handful of famous moments. Teachers will judge it as classroom material, so it must be accurate and calm.
**Purpose:** show a biography as a timeline: one life from end to end, the places and offices as bands, a family thread pulled out of the politics, a speech read with its sources and links, the presidency and the war laid against each other, and the last night down to the hour. Then point viewers at the timeline and at building their own.

**How it differs:** Video 5 sweeps a country across two centuries; this one follows one person across fifty-six years. The life leads and the app follows: every control is named once, in passing, by what it shows about Lincoln.

**Production notes:**

- Narration is 496 words, about 3:35 at 140 wpm, in the voice of a good history teacher: curious, not breathless, unhurried on the deaths.
- **The timeline is the sample `publicity/video/samples/lincoln.ochx`**, built from `lincoln.mjs`: 126 moments (seven of them chapter headings), 38 durations, 40 relationships, sources on each (Wikipedia, and where it's the natural reference The Lincoln Log, the Collected Works, the National Archives, the Library of Congress or the National Park Service), the Timeline theme in archive colours, a colour per thread. Shots are captured from the real editor in full screen (`shots.json` in the video's folder) and shown in a browser frame; the infographic layer adds titles and arrows around the frame, never over the app's own text.
- The timeline's display is **Gregorian** at a fixed **UTC−05:00** (shown in the bounds caption), so Washington's times of day read as Washington's; date-only moments sit at midnight of their day.
- Tags used on screen: `home`, `family`, `slavery`; the others are `law`, `politics`, `office`, `speech`, `writing`, `war`, `work`, and `chapter` on the headings.
- **Accuracy guardrails:**
  - Only well-established dates (Appendix B). Where the sources give only a month or a season (the moves to Knob Creek and Indiana, the flatboat trips, New Salem), the moment's dating note says so; the narration says "in 1816", never a day.
  - Say "a timeline we built from …" — it isn't presented as a definitive history.
  - The Civil War band runs from Fort Sumter to Appomattox; the narration says "Lee surrenders", not "the war ends".
  - Quotations are Lincoln's own published words (Collected Works); attributed sayings ("Now he belongs to the ages") stay out of the narration.
  - Account and classroom claims as in Video 5: free, no account to read a public timeline; accounts are for people 13 and older.

## ACT 1 · ONE LIFE (0:00 – 0:41)

### Scene 1 · 0:00 – 0:23 · The whole life

**FRAME:** The browser frame fills most of the screen, showing the sample after **Fit all**: 1809 at the left, 1865 at the right, summaries along the axis, and beneath it the long bands: Life of Abraham Lincoln, Married to Mary Todd Lincoln, Lincoln & Herndon, the homes in a row. To its left, a vertical strip of three chips: "126 moments", "38 durations", "40 links".

**MOTION:** The frame rises into place; the chips tick in on the narration's numbers.

**OST:** "One life, on one line."

**VO:**
"This is a timeline we built from the sources of one life: Abraham Lincoln's, from a Kentucky cabin in 1809 to a funeral train in 1865. A hundred and twenty-six moments, thirty-eight durations, forty links between them, and each one names the articles and documents it comes from."

### Scene 2 · 0:23 – 0:41 · Where he lived

**FRAME:** Still the whole life. **Separate by tags** with "home" chosen: a track rises above the rest holding the homes as bands, left to right: Sinking Spring Farm, Knob Creek, Little Pigeon Creek (Indiana), Macon County, New Salem, Springfield: rented rooms, The Lincoln Home, and President of the United States. A callout over the Lincoln Home band: "1844–1861".

**MOTION:** The tagged bands lift into their track; each lights as it's named.

**OST:** "Where he lived."

**VO:**
"The bands are durations: homes, offices, partnerships, the war. Separate by tags, choose home, and the places he lived line up on their own track. Two Kentucky farms, thirteen years in Indiana, New Salem, Springfield, and Washington."

## ACT 2 · THE FRONTIER AND THE LAW (0:41 – 1:49)

### Scene 3 · 0:41 – 1:04 · Kentucky and Indiana

**FRAME:** Rejoined, the capture zooms into 1809–1831 under the chapter heading "Kentucky and Indiana": Born in Kentucky, Move to Knob Creek, Move to Indiana, His mother dies (1818-10), A stepmother, Sarah Bush Johnston (1819-12), His sister Sarah dies (1828-01), Flatboat to New Orleans (1828-04). A small age counter at the frame's edge: "age 7", "age 9", "age 19".

**MOTION:** A continuous zoom; the summaries split into titled moments as they spread; the age counter ticks with each.

**OST:** "A frontier childhood."

**VO:**
"Zoom into the first twenty years. The family crosses into Indiana when he's seven. His mother dies when he's nine, and a year later his father brings home a stepmother who encourages his reading. His sister dies in 1828, and that spring he takes a flatboat to New Orleans."

### Scene 4 · 1:04 – 1:25 · New Salem

**FRAME:** The capture pans right to 1831–1837, the chapter heading "New Salem": Arrives in New Salem, Elected captain of volunteers with the Black Hawk War band, Loses his first election (1832-08), Appointed postmaster with its band, Elected to the Illinois House (1834-08) and the long Illinois House band starting under it, Licensed to practice law (1836-09). An arc runs from the lost election to the won one.

**MOTION:** A slow pan; the arc draws in as "two years later" is said.

**OST:** "Lost, then won."

**VO:**
"In 1831 he settles in New Salem, on his own. After a spring as a militia captain, he loses his first election. Two years later he wins, the first of four terms in the Illinois House, and by 1837 he's a lawyer, riding into Springfield."

### Scene 5 · 1:25 – 1:49 · One thread: family

**FRAME:** Back to the whole life. **Separate by tags** with "family": the upper track holds Marries Mary Todd (1842), Robert is born, Eddie is born, Tad is born, Willie dies in the White House, and two short bands among the long ones: Eddie Lincoln (1846–1850) and Willie Lincoln (1850–1862). A callout under each: "not quite four", "eleven".

**MOTION:** The family moments lift into their track; the two short bands brighten in turn.

**OST:** "Pull out one thread."

**VO:**
"Pull out one thread: family. He marries Mary Todd in 1842, and they have four sons. Two of them are bands here, because their whole lives fit inside his: Eddie, who died before he was four, and Willie, who died at eleven, in the White House, in the war's first winter."

## ACT 3 · BACK INTO POLITICS (1:49 – 2:36)

### Scene 6 · 1:49 – 2:09 · Five years away

**FRAME:** Rejoined, the capture zooms to 1847–1855: the U.S. House of Representatives band (1847–1849), then the band Away from politics (1849–1854), ending at the Kansas–Nebraska Act; the arc from the act to the Peoria speech that October.

**MOTION:** The zoom settles; the Away from politics band is outlined; the arc to Peoria draws in.

**OST:** "Five years away."

**VO:**
"After one term in Congress he goes back to the law. That band, Away from politics, runs five years, until the Kansas–Nebraska Act of 1854 opens the western territories to slavery. Follow its link: that October, at Peoria, he answers it."

### Scene 7 · 2:09 – 2:36 · Read a speech, follow its links

**FRAME:** With **View mode** on, the House Divided speech (1858-06-16) opens in the reader over the app: its date, the tags speech and slavery, the notes, a Sources list (Wikipedia and the Collected Works), and "Related to": Lincoln–Douglas debates, and Speech on the Dred Scott decision. Choosing the debates closes the reader on a view of August–October 1858: the debates band with Ottawa, Freeport, Jonesboro, Charleston, Galesburg, Quincy and Alton drawn apart along it.

**MOTION:** The reader opens; a pointer chooses the related entry; the view moves to the debates, and the seven moments settle.

**OST:** "Every moment has its sources."

**VO:**
"Open a moment to read it. The House Divided speech, June 1858: what he said, where that comes from, and what it's linked to. The Dred Scott decision before it, and after it, the debates with Stephen Douglas: seven of them in eight weeks, from Ottawa to Alton. He lost that race. Two years later he was president-elect."

## ACT 4 · THE WAR YEARS (2:36 – 3:31)

### Scene 8 · 2:36 – 2:59 · The war and the argument

**FRAME:** The capture shows 1860–1865: the President of the United States band and the Civil War band beneath it, almost the same length. Then **Separate by tags** with "slavery" over the whole life: the upper track runs from the Lincoln–Stone protest (1837) through the Kansas–Nebraska Act, Peoria, Dred Scott, the House Divided speech and Cooper Union to the Emancipation Proclamation and the Thirteenth Amendment ratified, with arcs between them.

**MOTION:** The two bands are bracketed together; then the view widens to the whole life and the slavery moments lift into their track, the arcs drawing left to right.

**OST:** "One argument, a whole life."

**VO:**
"His presidency is a band of four years and six weeks, and the war covers almost all of it. Separate by tags, choose slavery, and one argument runs through his whole life: from a protest he signed in the Illinois House in 1837 to the amendment that ended slavery."

### Scene 9 · 2:59 – 3:31 · 1862 to 1865

**FRAME:** Rejoined, the capture zooms into July 1862 – January 1863: Reads the proclamation to his cabinet (1862-07-22), Antietam (09-17), Preliminary Emancipation Proclamation (09-22), Emancipation Proclamation (1863-01-01), with arcs from the cabinet reading and from Antietam to the preliminary, and on to the proclamation. Then a pan right through 1863–1865: Gettysburg Address, The Blind Memorandum, Atlanta falls, Re-elected, The House passes the Thirteenth Amendment, Second inaugural address.

**MOTION:** The arcs draw as each step is named; the pan runs right at an even speed.

**OST:** "Follow the links."

**VO:**
"Zoom into 1862 and the links give the order. In July he reads a proclamation to his cabinet and is told to wait for a victory. Antietam comes in September; five days later, the preliminary proclamation, and on New Year's Day, the real one. By August 1864 he expects to lose re-election, and writes it down. He wins. And at his second inaugural he asks for malice toward none."

## Appendix C · Questions this audience is likely to ask

1. **Why are Robert's and Tad's deaths not on the line?** Robert died in 1926 and Tad in 1871, long after the timeline's span; their births are moments, and each note gives the year he died. Eddie and Willie died in their father's lifetime, so their lives are bands.
2. **Why does it say UTC−05:00?** Standard time zones came only in 1883; the display uses a fixed offset close to Washington's local time so that 10:15 p.m. reads as 10:15 p.m. Date-only moments sit at midnight of their day.
3. **How sure are the early dates?** Days are known for most events; where the record gives only a month or a season (the move to Indiana, the flatboat trips, his arrival at New Salem), the moment's dating note says so, and the reader shows it.
4. **Can I use this in class?** Yes: import `lincoln.ochx`, or open the public timeline, then edit a copy or publish it from your own account. Students can separate by any tag (law, speech, war) for a thread of their own.
5. **Isn't the war over at Appomattox?** Lee's surrender is the conventional end, and the band ends there; other Confederate forces surrendered in the weeks that followed, and the note says so.
